Whose Inference?

The same sentence means different things to different readers. This pilot asks whether an AI system can predict how a specific person will interpret a message, using an explicit record of what they know and believe, and then rephrase the message so it is not misunderstood.

One sentence, three readers

Each reader has a small personal knowledge base (PKB) of what they know and believe.

Editing one belief in a reader's PKB changes predictions for that concept and leaves the others alone in 98.5% of cases. But the model often reads the right PKB entry and still answers from its own, correct knowledge instead of the reader's perspective. A larger model does this less.

Rephrasing so the message lands

The same example, rewritten for the teacher who believes p = P(null hypothesis). Score = how likely an independent model listener (GPT-4.1) thinks this reader understands it correctly.

OriginalTwo teaching methods were compared with a t-test, which gave p = .03.0.00
Plain rewriteA t-test was used to compare two teaching methods, and the result showed a p-value of 0.03.0.00
RSA choiceA t-test was used to compare two teaching methods, resulting in a p-value of .03. This means the difference between the methods is likely significant, but it doesn't mean there's a 97% chance the difference is real.1.00
Across 18 at-risk cases the picture is mixed: selecting messages with a simulated listener helped only when that listener was strong, and different model listeners disagreed sharply about the same texts. RSA is only as good as its listener model, and testing it properly needs human readers.

What this suggests

  1. Interpretation depends on the reader. Measuring it properly needs NLI judgements from people with known knowledge states.
  2. Retrieved PKBs help, but LLMs default to their own perspective. Making them faithfully take the reader's perspective is an open problem.
  3. Cooperative generation inherits the quality of its listener model; validated listener models are the bottleneck.
  4. In a small model, the line between “what the user believes” and “what is true” is localisable and steerable. Whether this holds at scale is the next question.

Limitations: tiny self-made datasets, idealised reader profiles, LLM listeners instead of people, and a 0.5B model for the interpretability part. Details and references in the full report.
